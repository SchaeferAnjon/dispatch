# Teaching principles backed by evidence (short version)

Each: the principle in one sentence → what to do in explanations and pages → a counter-example → the source. The examples come from different subjects on purpose; they hold in any subject.

## 1. Remembering: retrieval and spacing

**1. Retrieval practice**: actively recalling is remembered far longer than rereading.
- Do: after each small step, have the student answer a question, do a calculation step or retell it in one sentence; review by answering too, not by rereading.
- Counter-example: three questions only at the end of a whole page; the review button opens the original text.
- Source: [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x) (61% vs 40% after a week); [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html) (retrieval and spacing are the only two of "high utility").

**2. Spaced repetition**: reviewing several times with gaps beats one massed review; gaps too short hurt more than gaps too long.
- Do: everything learned goes into the queue (for example 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 days), with shorter gaps nearer the exam.
- Counter-example: only wrong answers are reviewed; one right answer and it never comes back.
- Source: [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/).

**3. Interleaving**: practising different question types mixed teaches first deciding which method to use.
- Do: a set of mixed questions at the end of a lesson; review cards drawn across topics.
- Counter-example: five questions of the same type after each section, and a different phrasing defeats the student. Maths: adding and multiplying fractions practised separately, so when mixed, students do not know whether to find a common denominator or just multiply.
- Source: [Rohrer et al. 2020](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770) (61% vs 38% after a month).

## 2. Understanding: load and presentation

**4. Element interactivity**: the more new things to hold at once, the harder to understand.
- Do: no more than 2 new terms per paragraph; pre-train new symbols.
- Counter-example: one sentence with four terms seen for the first time.
- Source: [NSW CESE review of cognitive load theory](https://prod65.education.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/main-education/about-us/educational-data/cese/2017-cognitive-load-theory-summary.pdf).

**5. Pre-training**: get to know the key names and symbols first, then learn the mechanism.
- Do: one "symbol card" per lesson; each page lists only symbols appearing on it for the first time, one line each: how to read it, what it means, a real number.
- Counter-example: medicine: GFR and mL/min not introduced before glomerular filtration is taught, so students get stuck on units rather than the mechanism.
- Source: [Mayer's multimedia principles](https://ugc.futurelearn.com/uploads/files/7d/d6/7dd6188d-c343-4311-b064-ac98d2c95abc/Multimedia_Principles._R._E._Mayer__2020.pdf).

**6. Split attention and contiguity**: figures and explanations placed apart, needing back-and-forth, teach worse.
- Do: captions right next to the figure; numbers written on the figure; explanation and animation side by side, each step switching it to the matching state.
- Counter-example: the text says "see the curve at the lower left of the figure above", and the figure is two screens away.
- Source: [Chandler & Sweller 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_attention_effect); Mayer as above.

**7. Redundancy and coherence**: repeating the same information at once, or adding interesting but irrelevant content, crowds out attention.
- Do: numbers written in the figure are not retold in a paragraph; extra reading is folded; topic pages carry no recommendations unrelated to learning this page.
- Counter-example: a history lesson on the Treaty of Versailles inserts gossip about one delegate; students remember the gossip and forget the reparations.
- Source: seductive details have a moderate negative effect on recall and transfer, [Rey 2012](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details).

**8. Segmenting and learner pacing**: small parts at the student's own pace beat one continuous presentation; but giving beginners a lot of freedom (choosing the order, choosing what to look at) does not help.
- Do: one step per screen, the student presses "next"; the teacher sets the order.
- Counter-example: one long page with a table of contents, leaving beginners to decide which part to read first.
- Source: Mayer as above; meta-analysis of learner control g ≈ 0.05, [Karich et al. 2014](https://edweek.org/leadership/review-casts-doubt-on-importance-of-learner-control-in-computerized-instruction/2014/05); [Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006](https://caseresources.hsph.harvard.edu/resource/why-minimal-guidance-during-instruction-does-not-work).

**9. Worked examples, fading and expertise reversal**: beginners first study full worked examples, then steps are gradually left blank; too much guidance hurts experts.
- Do: four levels for one kind of question: full worked example → last one or two steps blank → independent → variations (reverse, changed parameters, judging the trend).
- Counter-example: maths: right after the chain rule, a derivative of a three-layer composite function.
- Source: [Worked-example effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect); [Expertise reversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect).

**10. Concrete before abstract**: first a tangible concrete example, then semi-abstract, then symbols.
- Do: before a formula first appears, calculate once with a set of real numbers.
- Counter-example: medicine: the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation first, then a mention that it relates to blood pH. The right way round: first ask "this patient's pH is 7.25 and bicarbonate 15; what has gone wrong?", then give the formula.
- Source: [Fyfe et al. 2014](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-014-9249-3).

## 3. Thinking it through: order and narrative

**11. Experience before explanation**: facing a problem or a set of contrasting cases before the explanation transfers clearly better.
- Do: the first screen of each topic is a question one can guess at; the explanation answers it.
- Counter-example: the definition first, then a worked example.
- Source: [Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf); [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/) (for beginners use the light version: a question answerable in 30 seconds, no long struggle).

**12. Predict, then see**: committing to a prediction first makes the surprise of the result memorable.
- Do: animations and experiments are written as "guess first … then drag to …".
- Counter-example: "drag the slider and see what happens".
- Source: [Brod 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8642250/).

**13. Narrative**: story structure (problem, conflict, complication, resolution) is easier to understand and remember than flat exposition.
- Do: one big question per lesson, each topic taking a step toward it; each page ends with a question the next page answers.
- Counter-example: fifteen topics like fifteen parallel little essays, each starting with "the last topic covered X".
- Source: [Mar et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8219577/) (75 samples, 33,000 people); [Willingham, The Privileged Status of Story](https://www.aft.org/periodical/american-educator/summer-2004/ask-cognitive-scientist).

**14. Small steps, many questions, frequent checks**: what effective classrooms share.
- Do: a one-minute review of the last lesson at the start; new content in small steps; aim for about 80% correct.
- Source: [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf).

## 4. Explaining it right: analogies, misconceptions, explanations

**15. Analogies need a mapping and where they break**.
- Do: write three lines: what corresponds to what; where it is unlike; what that difference leads to. One scene per lesson where possible, with fixed roles.
- Counter-example: biology: "a cell is like a city", and nothing more. Students come to think mitochondria "produce" energy (like a power plant making it from nothing), when they convert it.
- Source: [Glynn's six TWA steps](https://www.csun.edu/~vceed002/books/sourcebook/chapters/10-analogies/teaching-analogies.html) (step 5: point out where the analogy breaks down); [Richland et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1142103).

**16. Refute misconceptions directly**: stating the wrong idea and then correcting it works better than only teaching the right one.
- Do: write common mistakes as "you might think … in fact … the way to tell at a glance …"; quiz distractors use real misconceptions.
- Counter-example: physics: teaching only Newton's first law without mentioning the near-universal intuition that "motion needs a force to keep going".
- Source: [Muller & Sharma](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/6345) (dialogue videos refuting misconceptions roughly doubled scores).

**17. Self-explanation and elaborative interrogation**: explain why each step holds, ask yourself "why is it so".
- Do: after each step of a step-by-step explanation, a foldable "why this step holds"; at least one quiz question asks why.
- Source: [Chi et al. 1994](https://education.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/self-explanation_effect.pdf); [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266).

**18. Degrees of activity**: interactive > constructive > active > passive.
- Do: writing a sentence, drawing a picture, explaining to someone come before dragging and watching or reading once.
- Source: [Chi & Wylie 2014 (ICAP)](https://chilab.asu.edu/sites/default/files/lcl/chiwylie2014icap_2.pdf).

**19. Learning by teaching and the illusion of explanatory depth**: preparing to teach and really teaching both improve learning, more so the more interaction; people discover they do not understand only when asked to explain step by step.
- Do: a Feynman check needs a "beginner" who asks follow-ups and asks with a common misconception at least once; after explaining, write a few sentences in the exam's language.
- Limit: explaining fluently does not mean being able to write or calculate.
- Source: [Kobayashi 2019](https://metatoc.com/papers/112234-learning-by-preparing-to-teach-and-teaching-a-meta-analysis); [Rozenblit & Keil 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth).

**20. Concept maps**: drawing one yourself works best; studying a ready-made one helps too.
- Do: a concept map at the end of each lesson; in review, blank out links for the student to fill in.
- Source: [Nesbit & Adesope 2006](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm) (g ≈ 0.82 and 0.37).

## 5. Feedback and questions

**21. Feedback answers three questions**: where am I going, how am I doing, where to next; praise by itself does nothing.
- Do: the last sentence of any marking is always "what to write next".
- Source: [Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf).

**22. Multiple choice**: three or more plausible distractors, no trick questions and no "all of the above", an explanation after every answer.
- Source: [Butler 2018](https://source.washu.edu/2018/09/for-better-multiple-choice-tests-avoid-tricky-questions-study-finds/).

**23. Animation**: not inherently better than a set of static pictures; it must pause, step and be interactive, with key states also given as small static multiples.
- Source: [Tversky et al. 2002](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/871.html).

**24. AI tutors need guard rails**: AI that hands out answers makes practice better and exams worse; AI tutors designed on teaching principles (guiding questions, immediate feedback, not doing the work) work well.
- Do: do not do practice questions for the student; but when stuck twice on the same question, give the key step, so guidance does not turn into torment.
- Source: [Bastani et al. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html); [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/).

## 6. Plain words

**25. Conversational, speaking to the reader directly**: better retention and transfer (most clearly for lessons shorter than about 35 minutes).
- Source: [Ginns, Martin & Marsh 2013](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/designing-instructional-text-in-a-conversational-style-a-meta-ana/) (d = 0.30, 0.54).

**26. The curse of knowledge**: people who know assume readers know the terms and abbreviations too, and write things that are hard to understand.
- Do: have a real beginner read it, or check it against the list of students' real questions item by item.
- Source: [Pinker (APS Observer)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/the-curse-of-knowledge-pinker-describes-a-key-cause-of-bad-writing).

**27. A centre of gravity, narrative rhythm, no sprawling lists** (rules of experience, grounded in 25, 26, 7, 13):

- **A centre of gravity**: one point per paragraph, the most important sentence first, everything else serving it, the unused cut.
  - Wrong: "The kidneys perform many functions, including filtration, reabsorption, secretion, blood pressure regulation and hormone secretion, and play a crucial role in maintaining the internal environment."
  - Right: "Your kidneys filter about 180 litres of plasma a day, yet you pass only 1 to 2 litres of urine. Where does the rest go? Almost all of it is taken back."
- **Narrative rhythm**: set-up → question or conflict → turn → landing; long and short sentences alternate, and the conclusion stands alone as a short sentence.
  - Wrong: "The outbreak of the First World War resulted from the combined effect of many complex factors, including nationalism, imperialism, militarism and the alliance system."
  - Right: "In June 1914 a nineteen-year-old student fired two shots in Sarajevo. Why did one assassination become a war of five great powers five weeks later? Because every country had long since tied itself to others."
- **No sprawling lists**: do not lay out every aspect flat; no "first, second, finally"; no summary at the end of every paragraph; no "on the one hand, on the other"; no piles of adjectives and stock phrases (it is worth noting, all in all, let us take a look, it is not hard to see, crucial); do not announce what you are about to do; do not over-hedge (say certain things directly, mark uncertain ones once); no praise ("good question"); no fixed closing pattern ("without understanding this, nothing later will make sense").
- Reference: lists for removing an AI tone (based on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing", such as the open-source humanizer skill).

## Common myths (not grounds for anything)

- **Matching learning styles** (visual, auditory): no reliable evidence, [Pashler et al. 2008](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/). A student saying "I am visual" states a preference; everyone benefits from words plus pictures, but that is no reason for less written explanation.
- **Rereading, highlighting, summarizing**: low utility (Dunlosky 2013). "I understood it" is not "I remember it".
- **The Feynman technique itself**: no controlled study under that name; the parts that work come from learning by teaching and the illusion of explanatory depth (principle 19).
- **The more animation the better**: an advantage only when a process changes over time and can be paused and operated (principle 23).
